> The greatest contribution is not the work itself, but the system designed for others to do that work.

---

### Section 1: Context

A practitioner has become a master of a craft. Whether the medium is code, strategy or community organizing, they have a deep, intuitive sense of how to create value. Others see the quality, the elegance and the impact of the work. What they do not see is the *how*. The process is a black box, a series of skilled but implicit moves that live inside one person's head. The practitioner is a critical node in the ecosystem, and also a bottleneck: the system's capacity to grow is limited by one person's capacity to execute. Newcomers watch with admiration and struggle to learn. They can imitate the outputs, but they cannot reproduce the engine that produces them. This is what Michael Polanyi called tacit knowledge, known more fully than it can be told. The community's knowledge is fragile, embodied in a few experts, a flame that could go out with a single departure. The system is effective, but it is not yet resilient, and its competence cannot yet be shared.

### Section 2: Problem

> **The core conflict is Artisanal Intuition vs. Engineered Repeatability.**

The value of the practitioner's work is undeniable, but it is trapped in one nervous system. The very intuition that makes the work effective also makes the method opaque and hard to teach. A tension opens between celebrating individual mastery and cultivating the collective capacity of the system. The organization implicitly asks the master to keep performing, while its long-term health demands that the skill be given away. Newer members slide into learned helplessness, convinced they will never reach that level of intuitive skill, while the master carries the weight of being the sole pillar of support. Reliance on individual heroism creates fragility. The system cannot learn or adapt well, because its core operating principles cannot be inspected or debated; they are locked away in the minds of its artisans. The desire for scalable, distributed competence collides with the reality of siloed, embodied expertise, and the commons stays dependent on the very people it should be able to relieve.

### Section 3: Solution

> **Therefore, attention shifts from producing artifacts to designing the process that produces them, and that process is documented as a living, shared system.**

This is the fundamental step from artisan to engineer of systems. The primary output is no longer only the finished product; it is a well-documented, repeatable method that others can follow, critique and improve. The workflow is treated as a product in its own right. That means externalizing the mental models, capturing the decision points and articulating the principles that guide the action.

The aim is not a rigid, bureaucratic manual but a living document, a trellis that guides growth without strangling it. Such documentation acts as a shared memory for the collective, allowing knowledge to be transferred, debated and compounded. A personal "how" becomes a community "how", and what was implicit becomes explicit and open to improvement by everyone who uses it.

Documenting a method does not diminish expertise; it amplifies it, turning individual talent into a generative platform for the entire ecosystem. Newcomers gain a path to competence, peers gain something to examine and improve, and the commons gains a capability that no longer depends on a single person being present.

### Section 4: Implementation

Method documentation is an act of systems gardening. It takes patience and attention to the conditions in which knowledge can crystallize.

1.  **Start with a single workflow.** The whole body of practice is not the starting point; one recurring, high-value process is. It could be how a system failure is diagnosed, how a new partner is welcomed into the work, or how strategic planning is approached. The right choice is a process that is both important and still opaque.

2.  **Become one's own anthropologist.** For one week, the practitioner observes themselves carrying out the process, using a work journal or a plain text file. Every step is written down, however small: the tools used, the questions asked, the data examined. The *why* behind the *what* is captured. Why this tool? Which heuristic produced that decision? This is an act of self-ethnography.

3.  **Draft a first playbook.** The observations are structured into a clear, step-by-step guide with headings, checklists and simple diagrams. It receives a name, such as "The System Failure Diagnosis Playbook." It is written for a specific reader: a capable apprentice, or the practitioner at an earlier stage of learning. The guiding question is what that reader needs in order to perform the task at a solid level of proficiency. A short opening section on philosophy explains the core principles behind the steps.

4.  **Pair with an apprentice.** Someone who needs to learn the process runs the playbook while its author watches and holds back. The urge to step in is resisted. Where the apprentice gets stuck, what they ask and where the documentation is unclear are all observed. Their confusion is a gift: it is data showing where implicit knowledge still hides. After the session the two debrief, and the document is updated at once with the feedback. The apprentice becomes a co-author, and documentation turns from a static artifact into a continuing, shared conversation.

5.  **Give it a living home.** The document lives where the work happens. If the group uses a wiki, playbooks have a dedicated section there. If it uses a project management tool, task templates link to the relevant playbook. The documentation becomes an active part of the workflow, not a binder gathering dust on a shelf. A simple ritual keeps it current, such as a periodic review or a revision after every project in which the playbook was used, open to everyone who used it.

### Section 5: Consequences

When methods are documented well, the dynamics of the ecosystem change. The most immediate effect is **leverage**. Expertise is no longer linear; it scales through others. The practitioner is freed from being the primary doer and can take up the roles of coach, architect and strategist. The system becomes more **resilient**: it does not fracture when a key individual leaves. Knowledge is held in the commons, not only in the heads of heroes, and newcomers gain a fair path into competence.

This transition is not without its own challenges. A documented method can easily decay into **rigid bureaucracy**. If the living quality is lost, the playbook becomes a set of rules to be followed blindly, stifling innovation and adaptation to context. It can create a false sense of security, in which adherence to the process is valued more than the quality of the outcome. There is also the **documentation trap**, in which a team spends more time documenting the work than doing it. The map is not the territory, and too much emphasis on process can cost the intuitive, creative leap. Scaled competence has to be balanced with continued respect for artisanal mastery and with the judgment to know when to depart from the playbook.

### Section 6: Cognitive Era

When people work with AI agents, method documentation becomes more critical still. Documentation that was written only for people to read is also read by **machines that execute it**. A well-documented process is then more than a guide; it is a programmable script for a digital agent. An agent can read the playbook for triaging incoming requests, perform the first steps and hand over to a person only when the documented criteria are met. Documentation turns from passive knowledge transfer into active operational leverage.

Collaboration with capable AI partners also demands clarity about methods. An agent cannot learn from implicit, intuitive skill. It learns from clear, structured material, and a documented method is exactly that. Documenting a process creates the curriculum from which future machine collaborators learn. This forces a higher level of rigor in the thinking itself: "a feeling about this" becomes "these are the five factors evaluated, and this is the weight each one carries." Documenting for a machine forces people to engineer their own intelligence. The opportunity is that a commons can share its methods with people and agents alike, at a scale no apprenticeship could reach.

The risk is that the documented method becomes the only method. An agent executes the playbook faithfully, blind spots included, and the human judgment that once noticed when to deviate is no longer exercised. A playbook written for agents therefore names its own limits and the points at which a person decides.

### Section 7: Vitality

A vital system of method documentation feels like a well-tended garden, not a concrete parking lot. The signs of life are everywhere. The documents carry a recent "last updated" date. The change logs show contributions from a wide range of members, not from a single process owner. In meetings, people refer to the playbooks not as rules but as shared reference points: "The client kickoff playbook says success measures are defined at this stage. Does that still fit this project?" There is healthy, respectful debate about the methods themselves, with proposals for improvement and room for experiment.

Decay smells of stale air and neglect. It looks like a wiki with broken links and pages untouched for years. It sounds like "There is a process for that, but nobody actually follows it." In a decaying system, the documentation is seen as a bureaucratic burden, an artifact produced to satisfy a manager and then ignored. The methods fossilize, detached from how the work is actually done. The system has lost its ability to learn, and the gap between the documented process and the lived process widens until the documentation becomes a fiction, a hollow monument to a way of working that no longer exists. Vitality is in the updating, the debating and the living use of the method.

A diagnostic question: could a newcomer carry out the most important process of this commons from its documentation alone, and would they be invited to improve it?
