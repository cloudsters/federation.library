> Gather trusted guides from different fields and backgrounds to help you think through big decisions and navigate change. You learn from them, they learn from you.

---

### Section 1: Context

Every practitioner lives within networks of knowledge and experience: people who have walked similar paths, peers who see patterns differently, people who keep their integrity under pressure, and those the practitioner is beginning to teach. Most of these relationships stay ad hoc, episodic or ornamental. Help arrives when someone happens to ask, and fades when life gets busy. Yet the decisions that shape a working life, and through it the lives of colleagues, clients, families and communities, are rarely well made alone. A deliberately gathered circle of advisors, people who know the practitioner well enough to speak plainly, who are strong in domains the practitioner needs to understand, and who hold values worth learning from, turns scattered goodwill into a dependable source of judgment.

Such a circle is a small commons. What its members share is a pool of attention, experience, candour and trust that none of them could hold alone. Like any commons, it lasts only when its use is governed: when it is clear who belongs, how often the circle meets, what stays confidential, and how giving and receiving remain in balance. The pattern draws on polycentric governance in the sense of Elinor and Vincent Ostrom, many centres of judgment rather than a single authority; on reciprocity, mutual development rather than extraction; and on feedback, regular, honest and integrated learning. No single mentor holds every perspective, and over a lifetime each person moves between roles, sometimes advised, sometimes advising, often both at once. The circles that people build for one another overlap and form a wider web of mutual help, in which judgment and experience travel further than any single relationship could carry them.

---

### Section 2: Problem

> **The core conflict is Isolated expertise vs. distributed wisdom / Transactional advice vs. reciprocal stewardship.**

Practitioners need reliable counsel through ambiguous decisions, yet most advisory relationships decay into sporadic contact or become ornamental: advisors in name, with no real role. A practitioner without regular counsel is shielded from feedback, drifts into insular thinking, or depends on a single mentor who is wise in one domain and blind in another. Several forces push in this direction. Scarce time discourages regular engagement. Without structure it is unclear which questions to bring. Fear of appearing ignorant prevents honest asking, and the wish to look competent turns conversations into reports of success. Advisors from different fields rarely meet, so their perspectives never test one another. And reciprocity breaks down when the relationship serves only one side: advisors give and receive nothing in return, and they quietly withdraw. Without deliberate cultivation the circle dissolves. Without diversity, blind spots persist. Without structure, feedback remains vague and is never acted on. Without reciprocity, the shared resource is used up.

---

### Section 3: Solution

> **Therefore, gather a small and diverse circle of trusted advisors and govern it as a commons of mutual help: each member holds a clear role, the circle keeps an agreed rhythm, counsel returns as action and outcome, and help flows in every direction rather than only toward the person who convened it.**

The circle is small enough that each relationship can be tended and diverse enough that perspectives collide. It begins with a map of the domains where counsel is needed: systems thinking and pattern recognition; values and ethical judgment; the practitioner's own field; resilience under pressure; the dynamics of organisations; and at least one voice from outside the practitioner's world, who sees what insiders take for granted. For each domain the practitioner looks for someone who embodies that wisdom, knows the practitioner well enough to be candid, and lives the kind of integrity the practitioner hopes to learn.

Membership is explicit. Each person is invited with a reason, a rhythm (a regular conversation, an occasional longer exchange, written notes in between) and a statement of what the practitioner brings in return: not only questions but observations, decisions taken, and honest feedback on the counsel received. A short charter names the shared purpose, the rhythm, the expectation of confidentiality, and the understanding that every member gains from the exchange. These are, at the scale of a few people, the conditions that Ostrom found in enduring commons: clear boundaries, rules that fit the situation, and rules made by those who live under them.

The conversations rotate between a specific decision or challenge, patterns across the practitioner's work, and a review of earlier counsel. Disagreement is welcome: when an advisor is unsure, the practitioner asks what the uncertainty is; when the practitioner disagrees, the reasons are spoken and the friction becomes learning. Above all, the loop closes. The practitioner reports what was learned, what changed and what happened as a result. This turns advice into lived knowledge and gives advisors the feedback they rarely receive.

Over time the circle becomes a holding environment: a distributed structure of counsel in which no single voice dominates and in which the collisions between perspectives teach as much as any single insight. Advisors are introduced to one another where that helps them, the practitioner serves in turn on the circles of others, and what is learned travels on to the colleagues and communities each member serves. Reciprocity keeps the relationships alive through the changing seasons of a life and a working life.

### Section 4: Implementation

**1. Name the real gaps.** The practitioner starts with the gaps actually faced, not those it seems proper to have: where decisions repeatedly stall, where they are made in isolation, where rationalising replaces reflection. These are written down, because a named gap can be matched with a person.

**2. Find people who have shown wisdom in those territories.** They need not be prominent or formally trained. Good advisors have weathered something the practitioner is approaching; reason differently, not only in their conclusions but in how they think; are trusted enough to hear hard truths and to tell them; and have a genuine interest in a reciprocal relationship. The circle stays small enough for every relationship to be tended, and it is checked for sameness: if everyone shares the practitioner's background, field and views, it is not yet diverse.

**3. Invite explicitly.** People seldom know they are someone's advisor unless they are asked. The invitation says what is meant: a regular rhythm, real questions rather than polished presentations, confidentiality, and a commitment to be useful in return. A specific rhythm, agreed with each person, protects the relationship from vagueness, which is what most often lets such relationships fade.

**4. Map the circle.** A simple overview shows which person holds which perspective, where perspectives overlap, which is a strength, and where the practitioner is still blind, which is a gap to fill. The map is revisited as needs change, and members may leave with gratitude when their season in the circle ends.

**5. Keep a light ritual.** Before major decisions, and at agreed intervals, the practitioner writes a short update on what is being navigated and sends it to the advisors it concerns. Conversations bring unfinished thinking, ask for pushback, and listen for patterns across advisors. From time to time, where members agree, two or more advisors meet together on a shared question, so that their perspectives test one another directly.

**6. Tend reciprocity.** The practitioner asks what advisors are navigating, offers perspective and connections, remembers what matters to them, and reports how their counsel landed. Help is returned not only to the advisors but onward: the practitioner serves on the circles of others and passes on what was received. A circle in which help flows only one way is consuming its commons rather than sustaining it.

**7. Keep brief notes, not files.** Trust and conversation cannot be templated, and confidences are not written into records others might read. A brief private note after each conversation, one thing that shifted and one question still open, is enough to show over time how the circle is changing the practitioner's judgment and where it needs renewal.

### Section 5: Consequences

**What flourishes:**
The practitioner gains a distributed defence against blind spots. Each advisor sees angles the others miss, and together they offer a place where thinking aloud is possible without performing certainty. Decisions slow down in a good way, becoming less reactive and more considered, and dependence on any single mentor's framework fades. The advisors gain as well: a view into another field, the satisfaction of seeing their counsel tested in practice, a sounding board for their own questions, and connections to one another. The people around the practitioner, colleagues, clients, a team or a family, benefit from better decisions and see what it looks like to seek counsel rather than hoard certainty. Over time many of these relationships deepen into friendship, and the circles people build for each other knit into a wider web of mutual help.

**What risks emerge:**
A circle can become advisory theatre: impressive names gathered and rarely met. Time pressure erodes the practice, and conversations are held unprepared or cancelled. The practitioner may start seeking permission instead of thinking, or become attached to one advisor's view. Some advisors drift into reassurance rather than challenge, and support is mistaken for honest feedback. Too many voices pulling in different directions can produce paralysis instead of clarity. Confidences shared in the circle can leak or be misused, which destroys trust quickly. A homogeneous circle confirms existing views and calls it counsel. And when reciprocity runs one way, resentment builds quietly and advisors withdraw. The pattern holds only when it is tended as a relationship among equals, not as the extraction of a resource.

### Section 6: Cognitive Era

AI changes what the circle does, not whether it is needed. When machines handle much of the search for information, the finding of patterns and the synthesis of data, advisors shift from being sources of knowledge to being partners in judgment. They help decide which problems are worth solving, which questions to put to a machine, and how to weigh its answers in ethical and human terms. They notice when a tool is being used to rationalise a decision rather than to make it.

The circle also checks machine-shaped thinking. A practitioner who consults only AI systems loses the friction and surprise of human perspective, and the accountability of explaining a choice to someone who will remember it. Advisors push back in ways that matter: from emotion, intuition and lived experience, and from a stake in the relationship that no system has.

New roles may appear in the circle: someone who helps separate signal from noise in a flood of generated material, someone who questions technological solutionism, someone who keeps the human cost of efficiency in view. Written, asynchronous exchange becomes easier, which lets advisors in other places and time zones take part more fully. AI can help prepare for conversations, condensing earlier notes or drafting the update, as long as what advisors receive is the practitioner's own thinking and what they share in confidence is not passed to systems they did not agree to.

The deeper shift: where information is abundant, the scarce resource is judgment, together with the trust that makes candour possible. A circle of advisors remains one of the few places where judgment is formed in relationship and returned in kind, and it grows more valuable as more of daily life passes through systems designed to capture attention and defer agency.

### Section 7: Vitality

**Signs of life:**
Advisors make contact between scheduled conversations because something reminded them of the practitioner. Learning is shared with them naturally, not as an obligation to report. Conversations surprise; no one can predict where they will go. The practitioner's thinking shifts, and an advisor's way of reasoning comes to mind when the advisor is not present. Reciprocity is visible: the practitioner has helped advisors with something real, and advisors have met and helped one another. New members join through introductions from existing ones. Failures are told in the circle, not hidden from it.

**Signs of decay:**
A year passes without a conversation with someone on the list, or conversations happen without preparation. Meetings feel obligatory or performative: highlights are presented instead of confusion shared. Permission is sought instead of perspective. An advisor has become a cheerleader, and the practitioner feels soothed but not challenged. Advice is taken without thought or ignored entirely. Help flows only toward the practitioner. Everyone in the circle thinks alike. Meetings are dreaded.

**A diagnostic question:**
*If you had a hard failure tomorrow, a decision that cost you something, whom in your circle would you call, and what would you tell them? Would they say something that actually changes how you see it, and when did you last do the same for one of them?* If the honest answer is no one, the circle is not yet alive.
